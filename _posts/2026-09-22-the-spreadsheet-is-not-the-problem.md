---
title: The spreadsheet is not the problem
topic: Process
summary: Every new client tells me they need to get off spreadsheets. Usually they need to get off something else.
---

Almost every first call starts the same way. "We're running the whole business out of spreadsheets, and it's falling apart." I nod, take notes, and then spend the next week finding out that the spreadsheet is fine.

## What breaks is the handoff

A spreadsheet is a perfectly good place to keep a list. Where things go wrong is the moment that list has to become something else: a bake sheet, an invoice, a route, a schedule. That step usually lives in one person's head, and it happens late at night or first thing in the morning.

When I map a workflow, I'm looking for those handoffs. A few questions find them quickly:

- Who copies information from one place to another, and how often?
- What happens when that person is on vacation?
- Which mistakes cost money, and which only cost time?

## Replace the handoff, not the sheet

The cheapest fix is often to leave the spreadsheet exactly where it is and automate the step after it. One client kept their pricing sheet for two more years because the estimators loved it. We just stopped asking a human to retype it into QuickBooks.

> The goal isn't modern software. The goal is that nobody has to stay late to make Tuesday work.

## When to actually move off

There are real limits. Once more than three people edit the same sheet at the same time, or the sheet starts acting like a database with lookups across tabs, it's time. But that's a conclusion you reach after mapping the work, not a starting assumption.
